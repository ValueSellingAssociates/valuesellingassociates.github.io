You've been working this account for about six months. It's a mid-market software company. Their product runs scheduling and dispatch for field-service businesses, the companies that send technicians out to homes and job sites. Plumbing, HVAC, pest control, that kind of work. The product is good. The companies that use it tend to stick around, and the people you've talked to clearly like it. Your main contact runs the team that handles renewals and account growth. They've been generous with their time, and the two of you have a real relationship. You've got a foot in the door most sellers would want.

How to use this account. This is an example, so you can practice the thinking. The account is one company, but the way you read it is what transfers. When you do this on your own accounts, you will look for the business issue and the problems underneath it that fit what you sell. The ones the executive already sees, or the ones you can help them see.
1

What leadership is saying

The view from the top

On the last earnings call, the CEO spent most of the time on one number: net revenue retention. It had slipped below the company's target for two quarters running.

CEO · Earnings call
Getting net revenue retention back above 95% by the end of the fiscal year is the company's top priority, and the leadership team is being measured on it.

The Chief Revenue Officer was named directly. This is now her problem to solve.

2

The industry

The ground they're standing on

Field-service software is crowded. Two well-funded competitors have moved into the mid-market in the last year, and they're winning deals on price.

Buyers in this space used to sign three-year contracts without much shopping around. Now they renew one year at a time and ask harder questions. Switching costs have come down, and everyone in the industry knows it.

Revenue is still growing, but slowly. New-customer sales are roughly flat. The real change is on the back end: existing customers are spending less than they used to at renewal. Some are dropping seats. A few mid-size accounts have left outright in the past two quarters, which almost never used to happen.

Gross margin is healthy. Cash is fine. Money isn't running out, but the growth engine is stalling when it used to be reliable.

4

What they're doing about it

The move already in motion

The company has announced, publicly, that it's moving from seat-based pricing to consumption-based pricing over the next year. The idea is that customers pay for what they use, so a customer who grows pays more, and a customer who's quiet doesn't churn over a bill that feels too high.

Leadership has talked about this move on the earnings call and in the trade press. It's a real, funded initiative with executive attention behind it.

5

From your contact

What you've picked up
✎ Your notes

Renewals are getting harder to close. Their team is spending more time saving accounts than growing them. Support tickets from existing customers are up, and a chunk of them are about the same handful of features.

Mentioned almost in passing: they've been told to "protect the base" this year, and it's changed what their team gets measured on.
